# How these transcripts were made

1. **Two transcripts of each video.** YouTube's automatic captions were downloaded with yt-dlp. Separately, the video's audio was downloaded and run through OpenAI's Whisper speech model (large-v3, running locally on a Mac through Apple's mlx; nothing was uploaded anywhere). Whisper's text has punctuation; the captions do not. Three videos have no captions, so their text is from Whisper alone.
2. **Word-by-word alignment.** A script lined the two transcripts up. Where they agreed, the word stood. Small differences that change nothing ("cuz" for "because", "gonna" for "going to", a repeated word, an "um") were settled by rule. The two agreed on about 97% of all words; about 6,000 places of real disagreement were left across the hundred videos.
3. **Two independent readers.** Each disagreement went to two language-model readers (Claude Sonnet) who saw the whole talk and chose the reading that made sense in context, without seeing each other's answers. Where both agreed, that reading went in. A reader could also mark a spot unsure, or fix a word both sources had garbled, almost always a name; fixes were logged and reviewed.
4. **Disagreements between the readers.** The 475 spots where the readers disagreed, or one was unsure, were re-read in context by a stronger model (Claude Opus), and the whole set of settled spots was then re-read once more by Claude Fable, which changed 63 of them, mostly names and plain words both speech models had misheard. Every change is logged with its reason. Five spots stayed unclear in both sources and are marked in the text as "[unclear, listen: captions ... / audio ...]".
5. **Punctuation and paragraphs.** A reader then rewrote each merged text with proper punctuation, capitals and paragraph breaks, keeping every word in order. The only cuts allowed were "um" and "uh" and a stutter repeated word for word ("I I find"). A script verified that the word sequence of the finished text matches the merged source apart from those cuts, for every video.
6. **Names.** Names and terms both speech models misheard the same way (Pain Free You, Schubiner, MTHFR, Holter monitor, post-exertional malaise, Chronic to Cured) were corrected after checking the spelling against the speaker's own site or a web search, and each correction is logged.
7. **Who is speaking, in the interviews.** For the nine talks with more than one voice, a speaker-diarization model (pyannote, run locally) marked who spoke when in the audio, and a second Whisper pass gave a time for every word. Each sentence of the finished transcript was given the voice most of its words fell in. Claude Opus then read each labelled interview for lines that made no sense from the person they were given to (a question about the guest's story in the guest's mouth, an answer in the asker's) and corrected those; the corrections are logged. The names come from how the people introduce each other. Labels are shown in bold where the speaker changes. Expect an occasional wrong label on a short line such as "Yeah" or "Right", where two people overlap, and a sentence that holds both voices carries one name.

What this means for reading: the words are the speakers' own as two machines heard them, not a polished edit. Where a speaker misspoke, the transcript keeps it. Words both models misheard the same way can still be wrong, names especially. On-screen diagrams some speakers point at are not here.
